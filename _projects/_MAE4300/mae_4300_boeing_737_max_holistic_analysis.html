Boeing 737 MAX: A Holistic Engineering Ethics Analysis

MAE 4300 – Engineers and Society

Alex Jenkins · Fall 2025 · Cornell University

Introduction

The Boeing 737 MAX crashes in Indonesia (2018) and Ethiopia (2019) are often framed as a failure of a single software system: the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS). However, this framing oversimplifies a far more complex sociotechnical breakdown. This portfolio presents a holistic analysis of the Boeing 737 MAX incident, integrating ethical reasoning, systems thinking, stakeholder analysis, and lessons from automation ethics developed throughout Weeks 6–9 of MAE 4300.

Rather than isolating MCAS as a technical flaw, this analysis situates the aircraft within overlapping systems of engineering design, organizational decision-making, regulatory oversight, and market pressure. In doing so, it demonstrates how distributed responsibility and compounding assumptions can undermine safety even in highly regulated engineering domains.

Identifying Ethical Issues (Week 6)

Ethics in engineering concerns actions that have the potential to seriously impact the lives of others, either directly or indirectly. In the Boeing 737 MAX case, multiple ethical issues emerge when examined beyond the surface-level technical narrative.

  • Should safety-critical software be allowed to rely on a single point of failure?
  • Was it ethical to minimize pilot training requirements to preserve market competitiveness?
  • Who bears responsibility when automation overrides human decision-making?
  • To what extent should engineers challenge organizational and regulatory norms when safety concerns arise?

These questions reflect both individual and organizational ethical challenges. Importantly, they illustrate how engineering decisions are embedded within sociotechnical systems where ethical responsibility is often diffused across actors rather than concentrated in a single decision-maker.

Narrowing the Focus

While many ethical issues are present in the 737 MAX crisis, this analysis focuses on three interconnected concerns: (1) the design and implementation of MCAS, (2) assumptions about pilot behavior and training, and (3) the regulatory certification process. These issues were selected because they sit at the intersection of technical design, human factors, and institutional governance, making them especially consequential for public safety.

Facts, Assumptions, and Missing Information (Week 7)

Relevant Facts

  • MCAS was designed to automatically adjust aircraft pitch based on angle-of-attack sensor input.
  • The system relied on data from a single angle-of-attack sensor.
  • MCAS activation and failure modes were not fully disclosed in pilot manuals.
  • Certification authority was partially delegated from the FAA to Boeing.

Reasonable Assumptions

  • Engineers raised more internal concerns than were formally documented.
  • Market pressure to compete with Airbus influenced design and certification decisions.
  • Pilots were expected to respond correctly under extreme time pressure with limited system visibility.

Distinguishing between facts and assumptions is critical to ethical analysis. In the 737 MAX case, unexamined assumptions—particularly regarding pilot response and system redundancy—played a central role in enabling failure.

Stakeholder Analysis and Regulatory Context (Week 8)

The Boeing 737 MAX crisis involved a wide network of stakeholders with unequal access to information and authority. Core stakeholders included Boeing engineers and executives, FAA regulators, airline operators, pilots, and the flying public.

Delegation of certification responsibilities blurred the boundary between regulator and regulated entity, creating conflicts of interest and weakening independent oversight. Pilots, meanwhile, were placed in a reactive role, expected to manage system failures without sufficient knowledge of the system’s design.

This asymmetry of knowledge and power illustrates how organizational structures can unintentionally prioritize efficiency and profitability over safety, even in industries with strong regulatory traditions.

Automation, Black Boxes, and Ethical Responsibility (Week 9)

Although MCAS predates contemporary artificial intelligence systems, it exemplifies many of the ethical challenges associated with automation. MCAS functioned as a black-box system, intervening in flight control without transparent communication to pilots.

The system’s authority to override human inputs raises fundamental questions about autonomy, accountability, and trust. When automated systems fail, responsibility becomes difficult to assign, illustrating the “problem of many hands” discussed in class.

The 737 MAX case serves as an early warning for AI-enabled engineering systems, highlighting the need for transparency, human-in-the-loop design, and ethical governance structures before harm occurs.

Resolution and Lessons Learned

A holistic resolution of the Boeing 737 MAX crisis requires more than technical fixes. While redesigning MCAS and enhancing pilot training were necessary steps, they address only part of the problem. Ethical engineering practice must also confront organizational incentives, regulatory frameworks, and cultural norms that shape decision-making.

Engineers have a professional responsibility to protect public safety, even when doing so conflicts with institutional pressures. The 737 MAX incident underscores the importance of ethical literacy, systems thinking, and moral courage in modern engineering practice.

Conclusion

The Boeing 737 MAX crisis was not the result of a single failure, but of accumulated ethical, technical, and organizational shortcomings. By applying the frameworks developed in Weeks 6–9 of MAE 4300, this portfolio demonstrates how engineering ethics must be understood as a system-level responsibility. As engineering systems grow increasingly complex and automated, the lessons of the 737 MAX remain critically relevant for the future of the profession.
